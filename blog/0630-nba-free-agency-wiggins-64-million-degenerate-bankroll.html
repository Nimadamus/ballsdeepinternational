The Heat Just Gave Andrew Wiggins $64 Million And I Cannot Get My Sportsbook To Refund A $20 Same-Game Parlay

Posted June 30, 2026 · Filed under Degeneracy · Offseason Sweat Desk

It is the last day of June, the playoffs are over, there is not a single basketball game being played anywhere on Earth, and the NBA has decided to celebrate this total absence of actual sports by setting fire to a mountain of money in front of me. I am sitting here, a man who got told by an app last week that his $20 same-game parlay does not qualify for a refund because one of the legs technically settled, watching the Miami Heat agree to hand Andrew Wiggins a three-year contract worth $64 million to keep doing the exact same job he was already doing. Sixty-four million dollars. For the privilege of staying somewhere. I have begged customer service for a $20 credit on a bet that lost by half a point and got a copy-pasted paragraph about house rules. The gap between these two financial realities is the entire reason this website exists.

Free agency week is a special kind of poison for the degenerate, because it is the one stretch of the calendar where the sport gives you absolutely nothing to bet and a thousand reasons to feel something. There are no games. There are no box scores. There is no over to hammer, no first-inning total to sweat, no live line ticking down while you negotiate with your own heart rate. There is only a firehose of numbers, every one of them bigger than your net worth, and a brain that has been chemically conditioned to turn numbers into action.

The Money Is Real And It Is Not For You

Let me read you the receipts, because the receipts are the joke. Andrew Wiggins, a perfectly fine NBA wing, is signing for three years and $64 million to stay with the Heat. Over in Los Angeles, Deandre Ayton just picked up his $8.1 million player option for next season, which means a man decided that taking eight million guaranteed dollars was the smart and conservative move, and he is correct, and that is the part that breaks me. Meanwhile his teammate Marcus Smart is declining a $5.4 million option to go test the open market, which is to say he looked at five and a half million dollars and said, no thank you, I believe I can do better. I have looked at the change in my car cupholder and decided it was enough to load onto a betting app. These are not the same risk tolerance. These are not the same universe.

And the books know exactly what this week does to people like me. The moment the regular betting menu goes quiet, the futures board lights up like a carnival. Updated championship odds. Updated win totals. Over/under on how many games a team that just changed one role player will win in a season that is four months away. They put it right there at the top of the app, glowing, the way a grocery store puts the candy at the height of a child sitting in a shopping cart. It is not an accident. It is architecture.

An offseason win total is a bet on a season that has not been built yet, priced by people who do it for a living, refreshed every time a depth guy switches uniforms. You are not betting on basketball. You are betting on a rumor wearing a jersey it has not been issued.

Why The Dead Week Is The Most Dangerous Week

Here is the trap, stated plainly so I can ignore it in real time later. When there are real games, your degeneracy at least has a clock on it. The game ends. The bet settles. You win or you lose and then you are forced to confront the result and, in theory, learn something. Futures during free agency have no such mercy. You can lock up a team win total tonight, in June, and then spend the next four months watching the number drift, second-guessing, wanting to hedge, wanting to double down, marinating in a bet that will not resolve until basketball has come back from the dead. That is not gambling. That is signing up for a four-month anxiety subscription with a financial penalty at the end.

I know this feeling because I have lived inside it before, the slow-motion sweat of a wager that refuses to die, the same purgatory I wrote about when a rain delay left my bet slip frozen and I started negotiating with the weather. A futures ticket is that rain delay, except the rain delay lasts an entire summer. You do not get to find out if you were right. You just get to carry it.

The Grades Are A Casino In A Cardigan

The other thing the dead week produces, in industrial quantities, is grades. Every outlet on the planet is grading the Wiggins deal. Grading Ayton picking up his option. Grading whether the Heat won or lost the transaction. A man re-signs and gets a letter grade like he turned in a book report. And I want to be honest about why I read all of it: because a B-plus on a signing feels like information, and information feels like an edge, and an edge feels like a reason to bet. It is none of those things. It is content. It is a very smart, very entertaining way of filling the silence between seasons, and if you let it talk you into a futures position in June, you have let a hot take pick your pocket.

The honest read on Wiggins to the Heat, on Ayton opting in, on Smart opting out, is that none of it tells you a single usable thing about a game, because there are no games. It tells you that rich organizations are moving money around to other rich people while I sit in the cheap seats of my own life trying to get a customer service bot to acknowledge that my parlay was robbed. The grades are entertainment. The transactions are real. The bet you want to place on top of them is the only fake thing in the room.

The Free Agency Memo, To Myself, From The Version Of Me That Has A Checking Account

So here is the deal I am making with myself on the last night of June. I am not betting an NBA win total tonight on a team that swapped a couple of rotation pieces. I am not buying championship futures because a $64 million signing gave me a feeling. I am not locking up four months of anxiety for the dopamine of clicking a button while there is literally no basketball to watch. The season will come back. The board will be packed again. There will be a real over to hammer and a real game to ruin my evening in a clean, settled, same-night kind of way, the way nature intended.

The whole skill of surviving the offseason is recognizing that an empty schedule is not a problem the futures board exists to solve. It is a gift. It is the league handing you four months off from yourself, and the only winning move is to take the break instead of inventing action where none exists. Andrew Wiggins is going to be just fine on his $64 million. Deandre Ayton is going to be just fine on his eight. Marcus Smart is going to go find his number somewhere, because men who turn down five million dollars usually do. And I am going to be just fine too, specifically because I am going to close this app, accept that my $20 parlay is gone forever, and not replace it with a futures ticket that costs more and lasts longer.

Bet nothing during the dead week, read your house rules before you tail a single grade, and remember that the most expensive bets ever placed were the ones made out of boredom while waiting for real games to come back. Now if you will excuse me, I am going to go not bet anything, which I will absolutely do right after I take exactly one look at the updated win totals, just to see the number, like a responsible adult who has learned nothing.
