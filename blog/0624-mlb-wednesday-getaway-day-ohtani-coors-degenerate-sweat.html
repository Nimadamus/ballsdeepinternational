Getaway Day Handed Me Ohtani Pitching And A Man Trying To Survive Coors, So Naturally I Built A Parlay Out Of Hope

Posted June 24, 2026 · Filed under Degeneracy · Midweek Sweat Desk

There is a special kind of danger to a Wednesday. Nobody warns you about it. The whole degenerate culture is built around the weekend, the marquee Sunday Night Baseball, the Saturday slate that takes your entire day hostage. But the Wednesday getaway-day card is the assassin nobody sees coming, because it walks in dressed as a quiet little afternoon and leaves with your rent money. Today is one of those Wednesdays, and the schedule makers loaded it with exactly the kind of bait that turns a man with a job and a calendar reminder into a guy refreshing a box score in a bathroom stall at 2:15 in the afternoon.

I want to be clear about the structure of the trap before I walk into it anyway. Getaway day is when teams play the last game of a series, usually a matinee, because somebody has a flight to catch. The games start early. They overlap with the part of the day where you are supposed to be a functioning adult. And because they are day games, your brain files them under harmless, the way you file a single beer at lunch under harmless, right up until the point where it absolutely is not.

They Are Letting Ohtani Pitch Again, And I Have Feelings

Let us start with the headliner that broke my composure before I finished my coffee. The Los Angeles Dodgers, who sit at a frankly rude 51-29 and own the best record in the sport, are sending Shohei Ohtani to the mound against the Minnesota Twins. Ohtani. Pitching. Carrying a 7-2 record and a 1.47 ERA, a number so small it looks like a typo, a number that belongs on a thermostat and not on a starting pitcher. The man hits like a video game on the easiest difficulty and then, on his throwing days, turns into the kind of arm that makes the opposing dugout consider a career change.

Here is what a 1.47 ERA does to the degenerate mind. It whispers that the Twins, a 34-46 club, are not going to score, which is probably true, and then it tries to sell you on the idea that this is free money, which is a lie. Everybody can see the 1.47. The Twins team total is sitting on the floor for a reason. There is no secret here. You are not the only person who noticed that one of the best two-way players in the history of the sport is good at pitching. The line already ate that information for breakfast, and if you chase it like you discovered fire, you are paying the most expensive retail price on the entire board.

A 1.47 ERA is not an edge you found. It is an edge the entire planet found, priced in down to the decimal, and dressed up to look like a gift so you will overbet it. The best pitching matchup on the slate is almost never the best bet on the slate.

Somebody Is Going To Try And Pitch At Coors Field

Now for the comedy. Out in Denver, the Boston Red Sox are sending Ranger Suarez and his very respectable 2.93 ERA into Coors Field, the highest, thinnest, most baseball-physics-defying ballpark in existence, to face the Colorado Rockies and Kyle Freeland, who is carrying a 1-7 record and a 7.36 ERA into his own home park like a man carrying a wedding cake through a hurricane. So the matchup on paper is a four-run gap between the two starters' ERAs, which would be a slam dunk anywhere on Earth except the one stadium where pitching goes to file for hazard pay.

This is the purest test of degenerate discipline on the whole card. The smart, boring read is that the better arm wins more often even at altitude, because a 7.36 ERA at home is still a 7.36 ERA. The degenerate read is to look at Coors, feel the thin air in your soul, and immediately want to hammer the over until your account screams. The Rockies are 31-49, the worst record in the league, and Coors has spent decades convincing otherwise intelligent men that the laws of run-scoring are merely suggestions. Both of those things are true at the same time, which is exactly why this game is a sweat and not a lock, no matter which side your gut is screaming about.

The Quietest Trap Is In Washington

And then there is the game nobody is talking about, which is precisely why it is the most dangerous one for a guy like me. The Philadelphia Phillies and Washington Nationals are throwing Aaron Nola and Miles Mikolas at each other, and both of these arms are having the kind of season they will not be putting on their holiday cards. Nola is sitting at a 3-4 record with a 5.71 ERA. Mikolas is at 2-6 with a 5.47. Two starters, both north of a five ERA, in the same game, which is the baseball equivalent of two guys agreeing to a fistfight where neither one has brought up their hands.

The degenerate sees that and his pupils turn into little over symbols. Two batting practice arms, runs for everyone, back up the truck on the over. And maybe. But maybe is the word that funds the entire gambling industry. A crowded midweek board is not more opportunity, it is more surface area to lose on, and I have written that exact sentence to myself before, the last time I let a big slate convince me that one pending bet slip could hijack my whole afternoon. The disease does not care that it is a Wednesday. The disease loves a Wednesday. The disease has the day off and wants to spend it with you.

The Getaway-Day Memo, To Myself, From The Version Of Me With A Calendar

So here is the deal I am making before the first matinee first pitch. I am not parlaying Ohtani into the Coors over into the Washington over into some four-leg Frankenstein that needs every coin flip in the National League to land on its edge. I am not laying the Twins into oblivion because a 1.47 ERA gave me a serotonin spike. I am not hammering the Coors over because the air is thin and my judgment is thinner. I am not betting the quiet Nola-Mikolas game just to feel like I have a read on the one matchup nobody else is watching.

What I am going to do, because I am pretending to be a responsible adult who has meetings, is pick one game, size it like a person who wants to eat next month, place it before the early window starts so I am not live-betting from my desk like a maniac, and then go do the actual job that pays for the hobby. Getaway day is generous. It hands you a stacked board at an hour when you are supposed to be productive, and the entire move is to treat that as a warning and not an invitation. The Dodgers will be great again tomorrow. Coors will defy physics again tomorrow. The books will offer me a fresh and creative new way to lose money every single day until I die, which is the one truly reliable line on the whole sheet.

Bet small, bet once, read your house rules before you tail anybody, and remember that the quietest Wednesday on the calendar took more bankrolls than any Sunday ever has, precisely because everybody let their guard down. Now if you will excuse me, I am going to take exactly one disciplined look at the Ohtani line and then close the app like a grown man, which I will absolutely do right after I check it just one more time.
