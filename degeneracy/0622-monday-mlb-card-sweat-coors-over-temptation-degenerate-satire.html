I Built a Seven-Game Monday MLB Card and the Coors Over Is Whispering My Name

A field report from the middle of a Monday baseball slate, where every favorite costs full juice and the thin air in Denver is doing unspeakable things to my self-control

I Built a Seven-Game Monday MLB Card and the Coors Over Is Whispering My Name

June 22, 2026 | DEGENERACY WARD, MONDAY CARD INTENSIVE CARE

There is a specific kind of madness that only happens on a Monday baseball slate, when the football and basketball withdrawals have nowhere to go and a full plate of mid-June games arrives to absorb the damage. I woke up, opened the app, and found seven matchups blinking at me like slot machines that learned my name. Brewers at Reds. Yankees at Tigers. Royals at Rays. Braves at Padres. Astros at Blue Jays. Guardians at White Sox. And down at the bottom of the list, glowing like a cursed amulet, Red Sox at Rockies at Coors Field. I did not stand a chance. I have never stood a chance. A Monday card is not entertainment. It is a buffet, and I am the kind of customer the buffet quietly loses money on.

The plan, as always, was discipline. One or two games, a modest stake, in bed by a reasonable hour like a person with a job and a future. The reality, as always, was that by noon I had a position on every single game, a couple of team totals I cannot defend in court, and a creeping suspicion that I had become the product rather than the customer. The Monday card does that. It hands you a whole league at once and dares your worst instincts to skip even one.

Laying Juice on Every Favorite Like a Genius With No Memory

The first mistake is always the same, and I make it with the confidence of a man who has never once been correct. I find the favorites, the chalky ones, and I lay the juice like it is a public service. The Yankees on the road, the Astros chasing a number, the Braves traveling west to face the Padres in a yard that does not forgive lazy fly balls. I look at each one and think, that is the safe side, and I lay minus money to win a fraction of what I risk, again, the same way I documented in agonizing detail when I wrote about laying heavy chalk on a baseball moneyline, a piece I apparently wrote purely so I could ignore it six days later.

Here is the thing about laying juice across an entire slate. You are not building a card. You are building a daisy chain of small disappointments. Every favorite that holds gives you a polite little nod of a payout. Every favorite that gets shocked by a last-place lineup and a bullpen meltdown takes the whole ride. Stack six of those together and you have engineered a machine that can only break even on its best night and bankrupt you on a normal one. I know this. I have a spreadsheet that knows this. The spreadsheet and I are no longer on speaking terms.

The Coors Over Is Not a Bet, It Is a Religious Experience

And then there is Coors. Always Coors. The Red Sox in Denver against the Rockies, a mile of altitude under the baseball, and a total hanging in the air like a dare. Every degenerate alive knows the legend of the Coors over. The thin air, the carries that die at sea level and go over the fence in Colorado, the gaps you could land a plane in. You look at the number, whatever it is, and a voice that sounds suspiciously like your own says the four most dangerous words in summer baseball: it always goes over.

It does not always go over. That is the whole trick. The Coors over is the most famous bet in baseball precisely because the bookmakers know you have heard the legend, so the number is already fat, already inflated, already pre-loaded with everything you think you know about altitude. You are not finding an edge. You are walking into a trap that was built specifically using your own confidence as the blueprint. And yet, every time the Rockies are home, I feel the gravitational pull, the same hunger I described back when I confessed to an entire June of MLB bad beats. The mountain does not care about my history. The mountain just wants the over, and so do I, and that alignment of interests has never once ended in my favor.

The Monday Sweat Is a Full-Time Job With No Benefits

By seven o'clock the games start cascading, and this is where the Monday card reveals its true cruelty. You cannot watch seven games. No human can. So you watch the scoreboard, which is worse, because the scoreboard strips away all the context and leaves you with pure stimulus. A run in Cincinnati, a run in Detroit, a crooked number in St. Petersburg, and your nervous system trying to figure out whether any of that is good or bad for the tangled web of positions you no longer fully remember placing.

Every refresh is a tiny seizure of hope and dread. The Yankees go up, the Braves fall behind, the Coors game is somehow under the total through five and you feel personally betrayed by the laws of physics. You are not relaxing. You are managing a portfolio of anxieties spread across four time zones, sweating a White Sox bullpen you could not pick out of a lineup, defending a Royals position you cannot justify. This is not how a person watches sports. This is how an air traffic controller works a double shift, except no one is paying me and the planes are all my own bad decisions.

What I Learned From the Monday Card, Which Is Nothing, Again

The honest lesson is that a full slate is a stress test for self-control, and I fail it every single time. The smart move on a Monday is to pick one game you actually have a read on, bet it like an adult, and go live your life. The move I make instead is to treat the entire league as a to-do list, lay juice on everything that moves, sprinkle in a Coors over because the mountain told me to, and then spend four hours discovering exactly how many ways a baseball game can break my heart in a single evening.

Tomorrow there will be another card. There is always another card. That is the quiet horror of baseball, the daily-ness of it, the way it refuses to give you a single day off to recover your judgment. So I will close the app tonight feeling whatever I feel, and I will open it again at noon, and the favorites will be favorites and the Coors total will be glowing at the bottom of the list, whispering my name like it always does. If you want to keep limping through the ward with me, the rest of the Degeneracy wing is open, you can scream into the void over in Fuck All, or you can come all the way to the bottom with the rest of us in Gaped. The buffet never closes. Neither do I.
