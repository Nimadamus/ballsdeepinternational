Baseball Came Back After Four Days Off and I Immediately Bet an Under Behind a Man With a 5.33 ERA Who Has Given Up Nineteen Home Runs

July 17, 2026 | DEGENERACY WARD, SECOND-HALF RELAPSE DIVISION

Four days. That is how long baseball was gone. Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, a stretch of calendar with no games on it, which the healthy population calls the All-Star break and which I experienced as a medically supervised detox I did not consent to. I got things done. That is the horrifying part. I fixed the closet door. I went outside during daylight and my skin did not smoke. My wife looked at me on Wednesday evening with something I have not seen since March and I am fairly sure it was affection.

Then Friday arrived, the games came back, and within ninety minutes I had a unit and a half on a Dodgers-Yankees under 9 started by a man with a 5.33 ERA who has given up nineteen home runs. In Yankee Stadium. That is the whole story. Everything after this is just me showing my work like a war criminal at a tribunal.

Let Us Sit With the Number

Roki Sasaki has thrown 81 innings this year. In those 81 innings, nineteen baseballs have left the yard. Get a calculator, or do not, I already did it at 4 AM: that is 2.11 home runs per nine innings. Two. Point. One. One. The man is averaging more than two home runs surrendered per full game of work, his WHIP is 1.36, and I have decided that tonight, in the ballpark with the shortest right field porch in the sport, in the first game back from four days of rest where every hitter has fresh legs and a clean mind, he is going to be part of a low-scoring evening.

I want to be clear that I know how this sounds. I am not confused. I looked directly at a 5.33 ERA and thought "yes, the under." That is not analysis. That is a man walking into a house fire because he heard the acoustics were good.

The Rationalization Is Genuinely Beautiful, Though

Here is where the disease gets clever, because the disease is smarter than I am and always has been. I did not bet the under despite Sasaki. I bet it, and I said this out loud to nobody, because of Sasaki.

Follow the logic. Everyone sees the 5.33. Everyone sees the nineteen homers. Therefore everyone hammers the over. Therefore the number is inflated. Therefore the under is value. Therefore I am not a degenerate, I am a contrarian, which is the same thing as a degenerate except you get to say it at parties. And the total was sitting at plus money, and once a degenerate sees plus money next to a bet he already wanted, the conversation is over. Plus money on a coin flip is how they get you. Plus money is the sportsbook version of a stranger saying "you look like you work out."

I even built the responsible-sounding half of the argument. Gerrit Cole is on the other side with an ERA in the fours and a strikeout-to-walk number that is genuinely excellent, forty-seven punchouts against eleven walks. Eleven walks. That is a real thing and a real reason. Both these teams prevent runs at an elite rate. It is all true. It is all defensible. And none of it is why I bet it. I bet it because there was baseball again and my hands were doing things before my brain filed the paperwork.

The Four Days Broke Something

Nobody warns you about the re-entry. You do not get four days off and come back calibrated. You come back starving. During the break I found myself reading about the Korean league. I watched a documentary about a pitcher who died in 1974. On Wednesday I refreshed an empty scoreboard app three times in a row like a man checking a mailbox on a federal holiday. By Thursday night I was not evaluating anything. I was a coiled spring in a bathrobe waiting for a first pitch.

So when the board finally lit up, I did not shop lines. I did not wait for lineups. I did not do a single thing that the version of me who fixed the closet door would have done. I saw baseball, I saw a number, I clicked. The four days did not reset my discipline. They removed it, and then handed the controls to whatever lives underneath.

The Sweat Plan, Such As It Is

Here is my night. First inning, I will be fine. Second inning, I will be fine. And then at some point a Yankee is going to get a pitch middle-in and I am going to watch a baseball travel toward a right field wall that is, and I cannot stress this enough, right there, and my soul is going to leave through the top of my head and hover near the ceiling fan for the duration of its flight.

If it stays in the park, I get to feel like a genius for four more minutes. If it does not, my under is dead in the second inning of the first game after the break, which would be a personal record, and I will spend the rest of the night watching a game I no longer have any stake in, which is the loneliest thing a man can do on a Friday.

And the truly grim part, the part I want on my headstone: if this loses, I am not going to conclude that betting an under behind a 5.33 ERA was stupid. I am going to conclude that I was unlucky. I will say the word variance. I will say it with a straight face. And tomorrow there are more games, and my hands will be faster than my brain again, and we will do the entire thing over.

Baseball is back. Pray for me. Actually, do not pray for me, pray for the fly ball.
