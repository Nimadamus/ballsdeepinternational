Incident Report: Employee Wagered On The Golden Knights Before Reading The Odds

June 1, 2026

In accordance with Balls Deep International policy, all workplace injuries must be documented, and emotional injuries sustained while wagering count, per the ruling in last quarter's case Me v. My Own Decisions. The following incident is filed for the record. The employee in question, who is also me, did not consent to this report and has asked that it be destroyed. Request denied.

INCIDENT TYPE: Self-inflicted financial wound, sports.
EVENT: 2026 Stanley Cup Final.
PARTIES: Carolina Hurricanes (favored) vs Vegas Golden Knights (underdog).
EMPLOYEE POSITION: Golden Knights moneyline.
ODDS REVIEWED BEFORE WAGER: No.
ODDS REVIEWED AFTER WAGER: Yes. Immediately. With regret.

Let the record reflect the basic facts. The Stanley Cup Final is set. It is the Carolina Hurricanes against the Vegas Golden Knights, and the oddsmakers have installed Carolina as the favorite. This is the Hurricanes' first trip to the Cup Final since 2006, a genuinely beautiful, two-decades-in-the-making story that the employee has personally tarnished by getting involved with money.

Section A: Sequence Of Events

The incident did not occur because the employee analyzed the matchup and disagreed with the market. That would be a defensible business decision. The incident occurred because the employee placed the wager first and consulted the odds second, in that order, like a man who signs a contract and then asks what it says. The Golden Knights position was opened on pure vibe. The discovery that the Hurricanes were favored came roughly ninety seconds later and is documented in the employee's facial expression, which witnesses describe as "a man realizing."

The employee maintains he had a thesis. He delivered this thesis aloud to the office dog. The thesis was, in full: "Vegas always finds a way, the favorite is always overpriced in a Final, and an underdog in a two-team series is value." HR notes that "value" is the word employees use to describe ignoring the obvious, and that the dog appeared unconvinced from the opening sentence.

Section B: Aggravating Factors

The Carolina narrative is classified as an aggravating factor. A feel-good story this strong, a long-suffering franchise finally clearing the conference final hump and reaching its first Final since 2006, is exactly the kind of thing that turns an employee's brain to soup. Rather than handicap a hockey series, the employee attempted to fade a narrative, which is to say he bet against a movie because he found the trailer too inspiring. HR likens this to booing at a wedding for tactical reasons.

FINDING: Disagreeing with the favorite is not an edge. It is the employee enjoying the sensation of disagreeing, which is a hobby, not a strategy.

Section C: The Employee's Pre-Loaded Excuses, Submitted As Evidence

The puck has not dropped and the employee has already filed his alibis, which we reproduce here so the record is complete:

If the Hurricanes win, which the odds suggest is the likelier outcome, the employee will claim the favorite was "too obvious" and that he was "seeking value." If the Golden Knights win and the employee somehow cashes, he will claim he "called it, saw it, knew Vegas always finds a way." HR finds both statements to be retroactive fiction. The only honest entry available is that the employee made a coin-flip decision and dressed it as conviction. This is consistent with the employee's entire personnel file, available in the Degeneracy ward.

Section D: Root Cause Analysis

The true talent on display is not picking winners. It is the construction of a story, after the fact, in which the employee was always right regardless of outcome. He has a version of events prepared for every result. What he does not have is a single defensible reason for the wager he actually placed, which he opened with the confidence of a man reading the menu after he has already eaten. In a Final between two excellent teams, the favorite is favored for real reasons, and being contrarian is a personality trait the employee has mistaken for an analytical method.

Section E: Recommended Corrective Action (To Be Ignored)

HR recommends the following, with full knowledge that the employee will comply with none of it: check the odds before wagering, not after. Respect the line, because the people setting it know more than you. Do not fade a team purely because their comeback is too heartwarming for your bitter little heart. Understand that "the favorite is overpriced" is a claim that requires evidence, not a feeling.

The employee has reviewed these recommendations and declined them. He remains locked on the Golden Knights against the favored Hurricanes and intends to live and die on every shift of a Cup Final he bet backwards. This concludes incident report #2026-0601.
